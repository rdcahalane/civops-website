CESMII · i3X · Hackathon Kaizen

A focused program that solves one manufacturing problem — and makes it easy to repeat

A CESMII i3X Hackathon Kaizen brings a manufacturer, its own experts, tech teams, chosen solution providers, and facilitators together to solve one real problem. The goal isn't a tech demo. It's a working tool, tested on the real job, that leaves the manufacturer with a clear way to reuse it on other lines, plants, or machines.

This starts with the manufacturer's problem and the engagement they are buying — not a pitch about CESMII. It uses an open framework and proven practices that fit how most companies already run continuous improvement, giving teams hands-on time on a real problem so the value and the ROI case build while the work happens, not after it.

Two disciplines, one engagement

A hackathon compresses the build. A Kaizen makes it stick.

This program combines two methods. Together, they turn a known manufacturing problem into a working solution your team can actually support.

Hackathon

Design, build, and test — fast

A short, focused build. The team moves from idea to working tool fast — no long phases, no waiting around between decisions.

Kaizen

Start from the current condition, measure the result

Start with how things work today — the actual steps, not just the software. Test a real change to the process. Measure it against that starting point, then make the new way the standard — not just a one-time demo.

1 · Select and frame the challenge Operations before architecture

The team writes down how things work today before talking about the tech. That means the workflow, the systems involved, the data on hand, common problems, who decides what, current performance, and the cost of the problem. This step keeps the work grounded in the real operation, not just the technology.

The output is a short charter that spells out:

  • The problem
  • The owner
  • The baseline
  • The target outcome
  • The systems and information involved
  • The i3X capabilities to be tested
  • Security and access constraints
  • The validation period
  • The expected reuse opportunity
2 · Prepare the working environment Access resolved before the event starts

Before the event, the team checks that the systems, data, logins, test environments, and people are ready. The goal is to spend the Hackathon Kaizen solving the problem — not waiting on access.

This prep work can include:

  • Finding the i3X endpoints you can use
  • Mapping the assets, relationships, events, and measures that matter
  • Checking data quality and access
  • Setting test and security limits
  • Choosing which apps and platforms take part
  • Defining what counts as done
  • Getting reusable templates and dev environments ready
3 · Educate, build and prove Working sessions and decision gates

During the event, the team adjusts the processes and tools together to drive the desired result — not tech in isolation: who does what, in what order, and what changes for the person doing the work. The team can include plant operations, maintenance, quality, engineering, IT, OT, architecture, security, technology providers, integrators, and developers.

The work should produce:

  • A working connection through i3X
  • An information model people can actually use
  • A working application, workflow, or decision aid
  • Tested exception paths
  • Visible conformance results
  • Early feedback from operators
  • A clear record of issues and assumptions
4 · Validate in normal operations This is where the Kaizen discipline matters

A good demo doesn't prove a good fix. The tool and the new steps need to run for a set testing period so the team can watch how they hold up: reliability, whether people actually follow the new steps, data accuracy, how it handles exceptions, security issues, how much support it needs, and how the real result changes. The team compares that against the starting point, fixes what didn't work, and standardizes the new way of working — the updated steps everyone follows from here on, not just the tool.

5 · Define the next deployment Part of the value case, not optional

The engagement should end with a real decision on reuse.

The team identifies:

  • Which pieces can move to another site
  • Which parts of the new standard work travel as-is, and which need to adapt
  • Which mappings are reusable
  • Which parts only work at this one site
  • What needs to be made more solid before scaling up
  • What kind of support it will need
  • Which plant, line, machine, or app is the best next test
  • What can be shared with others without giving away private information
Not a conventional pilot

A conventional pilot asks whether a technology can work. This asks harder questions.

Did it improve the operating problem?

Judged on operating value, not a successful demo.

Did i3X reduce custom integration effort?

Judged on technical reliability and integration cost.

Can it be supported and reused?

Judged on supportability and portability — can another team understand it, deploy it again, without starting over?

Did the manufacturer gain a repeatable method — not just a successful demonstration?
Three forms of reuse

A useful engagement tests reuse at three levels

The third one is usually the hardest. Being able to move the tech doesn't help much if the way people work stays completely custom.

Technical reuse

Connect without redevelopment

Can the app connect to another i3X system without a big rebuild?

Information reuse

Interpret consistently

Can the app read assets, relationships, conditions, and events the same way across different machines and plants?

Operating reuse

Adopt without reconstructing

Can another site pick up the workflow, decision rights, exception handling, training, and support — without rebuilding all of it from scratch?

What the engagement is not

Eight things this is not

  • A generic innovation workshop
  • A vendor demonstration
  • An architecture review with no actual building
  • A coding event cut off from the plant floor
  • A standards seminar
  • A pilot called "done" just because the prototype runs
  • An open-source project that forces you to share private plant knowledge
What it protects

Confidentiality first, contribution second

The engagement protects your private information first. It shares general patterns, test cases, tools, and lessons only where that makes sense. Deciding what stays private and what's safe to share is one of the real outputs — not a decision made after the fact.

Proprietary vs. shareable, decided deliberately
Technology & procurement

We bring the tools, so IT doesn't have to make an exception

The software — and, when it fits the problem and we've reviewed it first, the hardware — comes with the engagement. That's the point: prove the idea fast, without opening a new procurement process just to test it.

One bill, not five

Education and research covers it, on one invoice, sometimes with part of the cost offset. No separate vendor contracts to negotiate before the work can even start.

Reviewed before it shows up

Every tool, and any hardware, is chosen for the specific problem and reviewed in advance. Nothing lands on the plant floor as a surprise to IT or security.

Standards-based, so it travels

The result is built on open standards on purpose. It moves easily into whichever solution provider the manufacturer already prefers — the tool that proved the idea doesn't have to be the tool that runs it long-term.

The real technology choice comes later — once the value is proven, the manufacturer picks the vendor on its own timeline, independent of what was used to prove it out. In some cases, a company's own innovation function uses this same process to give an emerging technology a fair shot: a small award or pilot, evaluated exactly like everything else.
The customer proposition

The manufacturer isn't buying a hackathon for its own sake

It's buying a faster, more disciplined way to answer several hard questions at once.

  • Can we solve this operating problem?
  • Can we reduce the custom work normally required?
  • Can we support the result after the initial team leaves?
  • Can we reuse the architecture and method elsewhere?
  • Can we learn enough from the first deployment to make the second one materially easier?

That is the offer.
