1. Purpose and operating premise

This playbook equips an engagement team to lead a manufacturer through a CESMII i3X Hackathon Kaizen. The engagement combines a focused build event with Kaizen discipline: begin with a real operating constraint, establish a baseline, build the smallest complete capability, validate it during normal operations, and test what can be reused.

The engagement is not complete when an API call succeeds or a demonstration runs. It is complete when the team can show what changed in the operating workflow, what support and exception burden remains, and whether the architecture and method can travel.

Engagement principles

  • The manufacturer owns the problem, decision authority, operating measure, and final acceptance.
  • The operating problem leads. Technology choices follow.
  • A focused build event is the center of the engagement, not the whole engagement.
  • Conformance is tested, not asserted.
  • Normal-operation validation is required before success is declared.
  • The team records failures, exceptions, support demand, and unresolved risks as carefully as the successful path.
  • Confidential plant context remains protected. Generalized patterns may be shared only within agreed boundaries.
  • The second deployment is treated as a design requirement from the beginning.

4. Qualification and path selection

The first engagement-team responsibility is to determine whether the customer is ready for a Hackathon Kaizen or needs a short roadmapping phase first. Roadmapping is useful when the enterprise has several plausible problems, unclear architecture prerequisites, weak ownership, unresolved security boundaries, or no credible path from a local build to a second deployment. A prior manufacturer roadmapping engagement is one example of an optional predecessor: it connects operating priorities, architecture choices, technology candidates, and a sequenced delivery path before committing to the first build.

8. Technical and i3X checkpoints

CESMII’s i3X 1.0 package defines required and optional behaviors and includes an OpenAPI specification, implementation guidance, an automated conformance test suite, an i3X Explorer, an official Python client library, SDK documentation, and an open-source MCP server. The engagement team should use these assets as working tools, not as presentation material.

10. Facilitation mechanics

Visible boards

  • Outcome board: baseline, target hypothesis, current evidence, and confidence.
  • Decision board: decision needed, owner, deadline, evidence, and resolution.
  • Issue board: blocker, severity, owner, next action, and effect on the gate.
  • Exception board: scenario, expected behavior, actual behavior, user consequence, and disposition.
  • Reuse board: asset, generalizable portion, site-specific assumption, documentation status, and next test.

Facilitator rules

  • Bring discussion back to a named workflow, user, decision, system, measure, or exception.
  • Do not let architecture debates proceed without a stated effect on the operating problem or reuse target.
  • Separate a defect from a design decision, a missing prerequisite, and a hypothesis disproved by evidence.
  • Time-box unresolved issues. Assign an owner and decision deadline rather than allowing the room to circle.
  • Use gate language precisely: go, revise, contain, or stop.
  • Record what did not work. A clean demonstration with a missing failure record is weak evidence.
  • Require the person who will support the capability after the event to participate before live validation begins.

Required measures

→ Track these live in the Validation Scorecard tool
  • Operating result tied to the selected constraint or workflow.
  • Time from challenge definition to working capability.
  • Integration and configuration effort by role.
  • Conformance result and unresolved compatibility gaps.
  • Exception count, severity, recurrence, and mean time to resolution.
  • Availability, response time, and data-quality incidents relevant to the decision window.
  • User adoption, trust, workarounds, and time added or removed from the workflow.
  • Support hours, release effort, documentation debt, and recurring infrastructure/model cost.
  • Reusable percentage of code, configuration, information model, tests, training, and operating method.
  • Estimated and then measured effort for the second deployment.

13. Year-two reality check

Year one can make everyone look capable. AI-assisted coding, subsidized model usage, concentrated expert attention, and temporary project support can make the initial result appear cheaper and easier than the operating model that follows. The engagement team must expose the ownership burden before replication.

Risk areaEvidence to collect during validationQuestion before scale
Code comprehensionNamed maintainers can explain generated code, dependencies, tests, and failure modes.Who can safely change this six months from now?
ExceptionsKnown edge cases and actual production exceptions are documented and tested.What happens outside the demonstration path?
AI/model economicsToken/model use, latency, fallback, rate limits, and current pricing are measured.What cost changes if subsidies or pricing change?
SupportActual support hours, alerts, incidents, and user questions are recorded.Is the support burden proportional to value?
Security and auditLogs, access review, secrets handling, model/tool boundaries, and response procedures exist.Can the enterprise defend how the answer or action was produced?
Vendor/open-source dependenciesVersions, licenses, maintainers, upgrade path, and abandonment risk are listed.What breaks when a dependency changes?
Operating ownershipPlant, IT/OT, architecture, and product responsibilities are explicit.Who owns the bill when the capability fails?

14. Replication and community asset model

The closeout separates customer-owned implementation assets from generalized community assets. No confidential plant context, proprietary process detail, or protected data is shared without explicit approval.

15. Closeout report structure

  • Executive disposition: scale, revise, contain, or retire.
  • Operating problem, baseline, target hypothesis, and observed result.
  • What was built and how it changed the workflow.
  • Architecture, i3X use, conformance evidence, and security boundary.
  • Exceptions, defects, unresolved risks, and support demand.
  • Observed cost, including labor, infrastructure, AI/model use, and ongoing ownership.
  • Reusable assets and site-specific assumptions.
  • Second-deployment target, prerequisites, estimate, and success test.
  • Community assets proposed for contribution and required approvals.
  • Thirty-, sixty-, and ninety-day commitments with named owners.
Appendix A

Pre-engagement interview guide

  • Which operating decision or workflow repeatedly consumes more time than it should?
  • What happens downstream when the decision is late, wrong, or not made?
  • Who owns the result, and who currently performs the work?
  • What signals exist before the consequence occurs?
  • Where does the team assemble context today: systems, spreadsheets, calls, experience, or vendor tools?
  • Which exception consumes the most expert time?
  • What action is currently allowed without escalation, and where do decision rights become unclear?
  • Which systems and data sources are required? Who owns access?
  • What security, regulatory, or labor constraints shape the solution?
  • What is the smallest useful result that a frontline user would accept?
  • How will improvement be measured, and what could falsely make the result look better?
  • Where should the result be reused next if it works?
  • What support team will inherit the capability after the event?
